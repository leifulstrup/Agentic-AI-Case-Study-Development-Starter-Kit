## I. December 2025: A Strategy Question at the Top of the Curve

In mid-December 2025, Derek Waldron sat for a podcast interview with VentureBeat and described a problem most enterprise AI leaders would envy. The internal generative AI platform his team had been building for over two and a half years — LLM Suite — had gone "from zero users to 250,000 users" in its first few months after firmwide rollout. "Today one in two JP Morgan employees around the world use it nearly every day," he said. Adoption stood at "a little north of 60% or so" of the firm and was still climbing "week after week." A personal-assistant feature launched months earlier as a minor convenience had exploded into roughly 30,000 employee-built AI assistants — the podcast's title called it an "Agent Economy" — and lines of business were spontaneously standing up internal web pages to share the best of them (VentureBeat, Dec. 17, 2025).

Yet in the same interview, Waldron delivered a verdict on his own success: "this bottom-up innovation as great as it is and exciting as it is won't actually fully transform a company on its own. Business is run on… long processes that cross multiple different types of teams" (VentureBeat, Dec. 17, 2025). If JPMorgan wanted to move the needle on end-to-end outcomes — he offered examples such as reducing the time to disburse credit or to onboard a new employee — "there also has to be a little more of a strategic element to actually rethink what the process itself will need to look like in a world of AI and in a world of AI agents."

The strategic question facing Waldron as 2026 approached was how hard to turn the wheel. The bottom-up engine — self-service tools, employee-built assistants, an "innovation flywheel" — was the most visible AI success story in banking, and Waldron himself argued its value was systematically underrated: "The democratizing nature of gen AI—accessible to the long tail and enabling federated bottom-up innovation—has more value than most people credit" (McKinsey interview, October 2025). But the top-down pillar — centrally driven reengineering of a small number of high-value, cross-team processes — was where he believed full transformation, and measurable cost takeout, would come from. It was also the pillar that would change what jobs looked like. Waldron had told CNBC that workers would shift from being "makers" — creators of reports or software updates — to "checkers," managers of AI agents doing that work (CNBC, Sept. 30, 2025).

The two pillars competed for the same finite resources: platform engineering capacity, senior management attention, change-management bandwidth, and the trust of a workforce that had adopted AI voluntarily and enthusiastically. Leaning harder top-down risked converting a beloved grassroots tool into an instrument of restructuring — with implications for the roughly 317,000-person workforce that CNBC reported the bank's own July executive retreat had wrestled with (CNBC, Sept. 30, 2025). Staying bottom-up risked what Kevin Buehler — the McKinsey senior partner who participated alongside Waldron in the October 2025 McKinsey interview — called "proof-of-concept hell": thousands of local productivity wins that, in Waldron's own words, "don't necessarily translate into cost takeout… these snips often just shift bottlenecks" (McKinsey, October 2025).

How should the chief analytics officer of America's largest bank allocate the next phase of an $18 billion technology program between the flywheel that had made LLM Suite a phenomenon and the process transformation that might make it pay?

## What to notice in this excerpt

**Every factual claim carries a dated source.** Count the parenthetical citations — VentureBeat Dec. 17, McKinsey October, CNBC Sept. 30. Nothing floats.

**The protagonist argues against his own success.** The tension is not manufactured by the case writer; it comes from Waldron's own words in the same interview that reports the triumph. That is what makes it discussable rather than a press release.

**No advocacy.** The closing question of Section I poses the allocation decision without hinting at an answer. The case ends at the decision point; the Teaching Note holds the epilogue.

**The integrity note is precise, not flattering.** It does not claim "all quotations verbatim" — because two sources are machine transcripts and one was editor-processed. Stating the actual position is the kit's standard; a claim you cannot support is the one error a reader will never forgive.
